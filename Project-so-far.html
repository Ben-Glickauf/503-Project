The data

The data set I chose is the Social Media Impact on Teen Mental Health data set. This data studies how social media use affects the mental health of teenagers. It includes daily habits like social media hours, sleep, stress, anxiety, and physical activity. I chose this data because of the normalization of what people are calling “doom scrolling,” which in lammens terms is spending hours scrolling through social media with no end in sight. Most social media platforms today all have a section for short, repeating clips and videos that are meant to hold your attention for hours on end. I myself have caught myself doom scrolling, losing track of time and becoming completely encapsulated by these short videos, often called “shorts.” What I want to know is whether or not spending large amounts of time on social media can influence your mental health, and whether other daily activities such as exercise, sleep, and real-life interactions can benefit mental health.

Lets change some of the character values to factors. I also will not be including addiction level nor platform_ usage in this model. I won’t be including addiction level because it is too closely related to the response variable of interest, depression label (a binary variable taking on values 0 or 1). I won’t be including platform_ usage because it doesn’t relate to our research question. If we wanted to measure which platforms are more closely related to depression, then we’d include it. What we’re interested in though, is how all social media usage affects teen mental health.

Model selection

Before we decide on a model, lets fit the full model and see which variables are significant based on their p-values. We’ll use a significance level of 0.05 for this model.

Hold up. reduced_model_1 actually has the lower AIC, even though it includes one additional predictor (academic_performance) that isn’t individually significant.

What does this mean?

Removing academic_performance made the model:

  • slightly simpler,

  • but it didn’t improve the overall fit. In fact, the AIC got a little worse

For this project, we will use reduced_model_1 because

  • It has the lowest AIC of all the models we fit.

  • academic_performance is close enough to significance that it’s reasonable to retain.

  • It adds only one coefficient, so we’re not sacrificing much simplicity.

  • It lets us discuss that academic performance may contribute to depression after controlling for the other variables, even if the evidence isn’t quite strong enough to declare it statistically significant at the 0.05 level.

Recap of model selection

We began by fitting a logistic regression model containing all plausible explanatory variables. Variables that were not statistically significant were removed sequentially, and competing models were compared using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC). The final model was chosen based on a balance between model fit, parsimony, and interpretability. Although academic performance was not statistically significant at the 5% level, retaining it produced the lowest AIC of 100.07, so it was included in the final model
